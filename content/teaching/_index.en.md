I have taught physics at UC Davis since 2023, as a teaching assistant and as an associate instructor.

## Philosophy

My teaching philosophy is the following: there are no students who are inherently bad at physics or bad at math. There are only those who, because of some prior bad experiences, lack of fundamental knowledge, or simply lack of exposure, have convinced themselves that they are unable to learn. This turns out to be a self-fulfilling prophecy: a student thinks they're bad at physics, doesn't bother to change their studying methods, gets a bad grade, confirms that they're bad at physics, and the cycle continues. The way to break that cycle is to encourage them to be interested (which happens when their teacher is enthusiastic), to believe in themselves, and to not shy away from the work. Sometimes all a student needs is encouragement; in other cases, they need to step back and relearn the fundamentals until they are comfortable enough with them to combine them into more complicated concepts.

The work of problem-solving, however, is essential. Learning is never easy; what is easy is tricking oneself into believing that one has learned something. Especially nowadays, with answers to most undergraduate physics and math problems only a few clicks away, there is extraordinary temptation for a student to take the path of least resistance and use resources online or AI to finish homework quickly. The average student is beleaguered by a heavy schedule and by course requirements that they have to pass (and succeed in) in order to get a high enough GPA to graduate and get acceptance or employment; systemic and societal pressures will tempt them to do whatever it takes to pass, and pass quickly. Unfortunately, precious little learning happens during this process. In my opinion, the only way to learn physics is by solving problems, which trains one to use techniques one knows in problems one has never seen before. The great challenge for the current era is figuring out how to incentivize students to do this themselves, since graded homework is slowly becoming less and less reliable as a metric. As a TA, the way to do this is to ask questions and give hints without giving the answer away, and spend time with students to answer their questions. For the lecturer of a large class, this becomes more difficult. However, I have personally found success in providing students with a large corpus of practice materials (with solutions!) for them to work through in their own time, emphasizing the importance of doing so oneself, then grading based on in-person exams. How the efficacy of this will change given the increasing prevalence of LLMs remains to be seen. 

## Associate Instructor

**Physics 7C: Waves, Optics, Quantum Mechanics, Electromagnetism.** Summer Session I 2026,
161 students across six discussion lab sections. I wrote and delivered the
lectures, gave the demonstrations, created and graded both midterms, wrote the
practice problems, and ran the course with five teaching assistants.

From the university's Student Evaluation of Teaching for that course, some praise:

> Out of all the physics lecturers I had, Remi's lectures were the best I've
> retained out of all the classes in the 7 series. He provided building blocks to
> the concepts we may not have any knowledge of, worked through practice problems
> step by step, and was always willing to backtrack if someone did not understand
> the material.

> Remi taught and explained physics 7C concepts in a way that was easy to follow
> and understand right away. This is mostly due to him keeping his lecture slides
> concise while explaining the rest in further details out loud [...] I
> appreciated how he always stopped every few slides or so to ask if anyone
> needed him to talk slower or had any questions, and it made me feel like he
> genuinely wanted us to learn.

> The first professor I have had at davis where it is VERY clear that he cares
> for his students and enjoys teaching. After our first exam, I felt very
> discouraged with my outcome, but after recieving his email where he explained
> the low averages reflected more on the exam and how it was written rather than
> us, it gave me hope.

> This instructor was phenomenal! His style of teaching was perfectly tailored to
> meeting the needs of his students and I loved that he asked his students about
> the pacing of his lectures.

> He was receptive to his students and I feel like actually took their
> suggestions into consideration and wanted to see them/us succeed.

Some criticism:

> The exams are harder than the material shown in class or DL which makes it a
> bit hard to predict what to study.

> The pacing of lectures was perfect [...] The only thing is I wish that he held
> more office hours. 2 hrs a week is normal for a regular quarter, but for the
> accelerated pace of summer session I would have appreciated more times.

> maybe make sure the whole class can see demo's
